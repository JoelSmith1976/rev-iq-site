The Weekend Download, Edition 5

Nvidia changes leaders, Microsoft goes quiet, an AI that only answers yes or no, an Australian team backs a famous black hole photo and a robot dog walks a real one.

Week ending 14 November 20268 min readFree to read

Welcome back

Welcome to the fifth edition of The Weekend Download, Rev-IQ's Saturday round-up of the most remarkable things that happened in technology this week. Five stories, a number, one from home and something to make you smile, each with one line on why it matters if you run, build or back a technology business.

It's been a big week. The world's most valuable chip company lost its founder, and much of the world lost its email. We cover both, because neither could be left out. Then we move on to the good stuff: an AI that only answers yes or no, a closer look at how our DNA works, and an Australian team independently checking a famous black hole picture.

The two we couldn't leave out

Nvidia's founder steps down

On Thursday, Nvidia announced that Jensen Huang had resigned as president, chief executive and board member, effective immediately (Nvidia). Huang co-founded the company 33 years ago and built it into a US$4 trillion business that supplies the chips at the heart of the AI boom. Chief financial officer Colette Kress is now interim CEO (CNBC via The West Australian), and the board will look at candidates from inside and outside the company for the permanent role (Good Morning America).

The resignation followed a week of reports about Huang's personal relationships. On Sunday, the board opened an independent investigation into reports that he had been "the target of a foreign intelligence effort", while saying it had "no reason to believe that either national security or NVIDIA secrets were compromised" (Nvidia). Nvidia shares opened down more than 13% on Thursday, then recovered to close about 4% lower (BBC News).

Why it matters: the investigation had already put a spotlight on Nvidia's lack of a publicly disclosed succession plan (Investor Daily). If it can happen to a US$4 trillion company, it's worth asking whether your board knows who would step in tomorrow, and whether your investors know that too.

Microsoft 365 goes quiet for most of a day

Early on Friday morning Australian time, Microsoft's Outlook, Teams and Copilot started failing for businesses and governments around the world (AP). The problem began at about 3:17am AEDT (HRD), more than 130,000 people reported problems with Outlook on Downdetector (AP), and the disruption reached 74 countries and lasted more than 17 hours before traffic was gradually restored (news.com.au).

The effects were very real. Some organisations went back to fax machines, and at least one company postponed its results announcement (PerthNow). Australian government agencies, including the Australian Federal Police, were affected (Reuters), and DFAT asked anyone with urgent sanctions enquiries to pick up the phone instead (DFAT). To protect delayed messages while the backlog cleared, Microsoft extended its email timeout window to 48 hours (MacRumors).

Microsoft has apologised and said: "This was a technical issue not related to cyber or AI / agents. The incident was introduced by an issue during an earlier network capacity upgrade that surfaced following a recent network change" (ABC News). It has promised a full post-incident report with the root cause (CNN).

A few things are worth keeping in mind:

  • Most businesses rely on a single supplier for email, meetings, files and AI. That's usually a sensible choice, but it means one fault can stop everything at once.
  • Have a plan B that people already know about. A phone tree, a backup chat channel and a list of key customer contacts kept outside your inbox cost almost nothing to set up.
  • Your customers will ask you the same question. If you sell software, expect "what happens when your suppliers go down?" to come up in more IT reviews. A clear, honest answer builds trust.

Why it matters: outages happen, even to the biggest providers. The businesses that come through them best have already decided what they'll do, and how they'll tell their customers.

The five

1. Google launches an AI that only answers the question you asked

Most AI models answer in paragraphs. Google's new Gemini Decision 1 doesn't write anything. Instead, it answers up to 1,000 yes/no, multiple-choice or rating questions about a document, image, audio file or video in a single pass, with a confidence score for each answer (Google). It costs US$0.10 per million input tokens, with nothing charged for output, and responds in 150 to 250 milliseconds. The confidence score lets software handle the clear cases itself and pass the uncertain ones to a person. Google says the model is nearly five times cheaper than its entry-level Gemini model (SiliconANGLE).

Why it matters: many business tasks don't need an essay, just a decision. Using the right-sized model for each job is one of the simplest ways to keep AI costs under control.

2. Google DeepMind looks inside individual cells

Almost every cell in your body carries the same DNA, yet a heart cell and an immune cell use it very differently. Google DeepMind's AlphaGenome 2 predicts how a change in DNA affects specific cell types, rather than averaging across a whole tissue (Google DeepMind). Trained on more than 100 million individual cell profiles across 68 tissues, it finds nearly twice as many DNA changes that switch genes on or off as the previous model. In one test, it reproduced in 30 minutes part of a result that had originally taken a year and a half of lab experiments (Dong-A Science). It's free for non-commercial research.

Why it matters: the move from averages to specifics is happening everywhere. Customers increasingly expect technology that understands their situation, not the typical one.

3. An Australian team independently checks a famous black hole picture

In 2019, the world saw its first picture of a black hole, a glowing ring of light about 55 million light-years away (CSIRO). Some scientists later questioned whether the ring was real or a side effect of how the image was processed. This week, a team of four from CSIRO published an entirely independent check in Nature, and found "a black hole almost identical in size and shape" to the original (CSIRO). To avoid bias, they trained their AI on everyday photographs, so it "had no idea what a black hole looked like" before it saw the data (The Register).

Why it matters: the most persuasive proof is an independent one. If you want customers to trust your results, find a way for someone else to check them.

4. AI helps a bionic eye work with the brain

Researchers from UC Santa Barbara, ETH Zurich and Miguel Hernández University in Spain have used AI to make an experimental bionic eye more precise (ScienceDaily). Working with a man who is blind and had a 96-electrode implant in the visual part of his brain, they trained a neural network to predict how his brain would respond to stimulation. The AI-chosen patterns produced the intended brain activity more accurately than other methods, and used less electrical current. "Ultimately, the device should adapt to the person, not the other way around," said UCSB's Michael Beyeler. The technology is still experimental, but it's a real step forward.

Why it matters: Beyeler's line is a good test for any product. The best technology adapts to the person using it.

5. Last week's new magnet gets put to work

Last week we covered the discovery of a third type of magnetism. This week, researchers at Brown University showed how it could make chips use much less power (ScienceDaily). Promising chip materials tend to stop working when they're made very thin, a problem known as the "dead layer". The Brown team found a way around it, keeping the material working at just a few atoms thick, and found that it also showed the new type of magnetism. "You can make this material basically as thin as you want," said lead researcher Lucas Caretta. It's not ready for commercial chips yet, but it points to future AI hardware that needs far less energy.

Why it matters: energy is now one of the biggest constraints on AI. Breakthroughs like this are what will eventually bring the cost of running AI down.

Number of the week: 900 million

That's how many galaxies are in the first major data release from the European Space Agency's Euclid space telescope, published on Thursday (ESA). The release covers an area of sky as big as 9,500 full Moons, captures the light of more than a billion objects and totals about 1.7 million gigabytes of data. And it's only 14% of the sky Euclid will eventually survey.

Why it matters: data on this scale is only useful if people can search and analyse it. ESA has made it open for anyone to explore, which is how a dataset turns into discoveries.

One from home

Sydney's Q-CTRL has become Australia's first quantum computing unicorn, raising US$290 million (A$425 million) at a valuation of more than US$1 billion (SmartCompany). The round was led by Salesforce Ventures, expanded twice to meet investor demand, and included A$70 million from the federal government's National Reconstruction Fund. Founded in 2017 by University of Sydney physicist Michael Biercuk, Q-CTRL uses AI to reduce errors in quantum computers and also builds navigation technology for aircraft and autonomous vehicles. As Salesforce Ventures' Robert Keith put it, "That software layer will decide whether quantum shows up in enterprise data centres this decade." The AFR noted the raise came despite a cautious global market for technology investment (AFR).

Why it matters: nine years of patient work, a clear software role in a hardware-heavy industry, and investors queuing up. That's a great Australian story.

Just for fun

A robot dog was spotted taking a real dog for a walk in San Francisco's Mission Bay recently (KTLA). The small dog trotted along beside its robotic guide, with someone who appeared to be the owner following close behind. The woman who filmed it said she was "surprised and yet not".

Nobody knows yet whether it's a new service or a weekend experiment. Either way, the real dog seemed perfectly happy with the arrangement, which is more than most of us could say about our inboxes on Friday.
