The Weekend Download, Edition 6

We actually got GTA VI. Plus electrons that pair up at room temperature, a battery that runs on vitamin B12, Voyager 1 reaches one light-day and 151 robots dance in sync.

Week ending 21 November 20269 min readFree to read

Welcome back

Welcome to the sixth edition of The Weekend Download, Rev-IQ's Saturday round-up of the most remarkable things that happened in technology this week. One big story, five more, a number, one from home and something to make you smile, each with one line on why it matters if you run, build or back a technology business.

For years the internet had a running joke, and this week it ran out. We start there, then move on to electrons that pair up at room temperature, a battery powered by a vitamin, physics that can prove where you are, a humanoid robot heading for the chip factory and a star that swallowed its neighbour.

The one we've all been waiting for

We actually got GTA VI

For the best part of a decade, the internet measured the passing of time against one thing. We got a new English king before GTA VI. We got an American pope before GTA VI. We got foldable phones, the Grimace shake and, depending on who you asked, fighting robots and flying cars before GTA VI (CNET). This week the joke lost its punchline. Grand Theft Auto VI launched on Thursday, 13 years after GTA V, and the internet held a small farewell for one of its longest-running memes (USA Today). Rockstar was clearly in on it: players have already spotted an in-game social media post complaining that "we really got barnyard blue games before RAC7", a fictional game inside the game (Hindustan Times).

The joke is gone, but the numbers are serious. Rockstar's parent company says GTA VI took US$3.79 billion in retail sales on its first day, which it calls the biggest entertainment launch in history across any medium, and it sold 32.5 million copies before release (Take-Two). That's nearly five times GTA V's first 24 hours in 2013, and more than the entire worldwide box office run of Avengers: Endgame, about US$2.9 billion (news.com.au). It also generated more social media posts than this year's World Cup (news.com.au).

It's not just big. It's good. GTA VI sits at 99 on Metacritic, level with only Baldur's Gate 3 and The Legend of Zelda: Ocarina of Time (ABC News). IGN's reviewer called it "the single most impressive video game I have ever played" (Al Jazeera). Not everyone is quite so breathless. ScreenHub Australia gave it four stars and found "large sections that are boring and too limited" before the game opens up (ABC News), which is the most Australian review imaginable. And for the record, it's rated for adults, so this one is for the grown-ups.

Our part of the world had a front-row seat:

  • New Zealand played first, and it had company. The game unlocked at midnight local time, market by market, so Kiwi players were the first in the world to get in (news.com.au). In the US, that was 6am on Wednesday, up to 18 hours before the local launch (VICE). Ampere Analysis estimates that around 2.1 million Xbox players took the hint and switched their console's location to New Zealand: 1.5 million in the US and 600,000 in the UK (IGN). With New Zealand home to about 5.37 million people (Stats NZ), the country briefly grew by almost 40%, at least on paper. And that's Xbox alone.
  • The NBN had its busiest second ever. Traffic peaked at 36.54 terabits per second overnight, about 20% above the previous record. The NBN says that's enough to download the 212GB game about 21 times every second (ABC News).
  • Plenty of people planned ahead. One Australian player booked four days off work and played until 4am (ABC News). Online, about two-thirds of "GTA flu" posts were jokes. Only about 2% were from people who said they'd actually called in sick (PeakMetrics), so most of you can relax.

Even the biggest launch in history wasn't perfect. Around 23% of launch-day conversation was about access or technical problems, with posts about frame drops and stutter up about 55 times on a normal day (PeakMetrics). Rockstar delayed the game twice, from late 2025 and then from May 2026 (news.com.au). On the evidence so far, nobody will remember the delays. They'll remember the game.

Why it matters: GTA VI is a reminder that games are now the biggest stage in entertainment, bigger than film. For technology businesses, the more useful lesson is about launches. Rockstar delayed until the product was ready, then launched to a market that had been waiting for years. Even then, launch-day performance was what people talked about most. Getting the product right is what counts, but the first day of using it is what customers remember.

The five

1. Electrons pair up at room temperature

Superconductors carry electricity with no resistance at all, but most only work at extreme cold, around minus 260°C. For decades the dream has been one that works at room temperature. This week, researchers from Utrecht, Leiden and Munich showed that the electron pairs superconductivity relies on can stay together at room temperature in one type of material (ScienceDaily). The catch is that pairs alone aren't enough. They also have to move together in step, and that's what's missing. "This suggests that the second key ingredient, phase coherence, is the bottleneck," the team said. It isn't room-temperature superconductivity yet, but scientists now have a better idea of where to look.

Why it matters: knowing exactly what's holding you back is half the battle. That's as true of a stalled sales pipeline as it is of physics.

2. A rechargeable battery that runs on vitamin B12

Researchers at the University of Córdoba in Spain have built a rechargeable zinc-air battery that uses vitamin B12 to help store and release energy (ScienceDaily). The vitamin has cobalt at its centre, which helps drive the battery's chemical reactions, and the team attached it to tiny carbon nanotubes to keep it in place. The prototype lasted up to 250 charging cycles. That's well short of a phone battery, but the researchers see zinc-air designs like this as a potentially safer and cheaper alternative to lithium-ion (Corti).

Why it matters: most energy breakthroughs look small at first. Cheaper, safer storage is one of the things that will eventually bring the cost of powering data centres and devices down.

3. Physics that proves where you are

Scammers often pretend to be somewhere they're not. Scientists at the US National Institute of Standards and Technology have shown that quantum physics can check. In an experiment published in Nature this week, two stations 195 metres apart used entangled particles of light and very precise timing to confirm that a third party really was where they claimed to be (NIST). The test ran 30 million rounds in about two minutes and closed the loopholes that let earlier methods be fooled (Gizmodo). NIST says the technique could one day protect infrastructure and banks and help prevent phishing, though practical use may be more than a decade away (Daily Summary).

Why it matters: "prove it" is becoming the default expectation in IT. Customers increasingly want evidence of who, where and what, not just a promise.

4. Atlas gets its first job outside car factories

Boston Dynamics' Atlas humanoid robot is heading for chip factories. GlobalFoundries will be its first customer outside the car industry, with testing at its plants starting in early 2027 (Manufacturing Dive). The deal builds on a long track record. GlobalFoundries has used Boston Dynamics' four-legged Spot robot for more than four years, and since April two Spot robots in its Singapore plant have walked about 900 kilometres and carried out more than 90,000 inspections, with a 99% success rate (Chosun Biz).

Why it matters: the humanoid got the job because the robot dog had already proved itself. The easiest way to win a bigger contract is to be excellent at a smaller one first.

5. Astronomers watch a star swallow its neighbour

For the first time, astronomers have followed a star merger from start to finish. In the Andromeda galaxy, about 2.5 million light-years away, a star slightly smaller than our Sun spiralled into a giant and merged with it (University of Turku). The team nicknamed the pair Big Bao and Little Bao. Once they touched, they merged in only about seven years, much faster than expected. NASA's James Webb Space Telescope looked at the aftermath just 47 days after the eruption peaked, the earliest follow-up ever of an event like this (NASA). Instead of exploding, the merged star seems to have settled down as a single, "rejuvenated" star (Live Science).

Why it matters: the team spotted something unusual in 2023 and kept watching. Patient monitoring is how you catch the big moment, in astronomy and in a customer base.

Number of the week: 24 hours

That's how long a message from Earth now takes to reach Voyager 1. On Wednesday, NASA's farthest spacecraft became the first human-made object to reach one light-day from Earth, about 25.9 billion kilometres away, after more than 49 years of travel (NASA). Australians could mark the moment at 9:16pm AEDT (ABC News). Ask Voyager a question and you'll wait a full day for it to arrive, and another day for the answer to come back (NASA). The spacecraft is having a data issue right now, but the team was still able to confirm the milestone from its carrier signal (NASA).

Why it matters: Voyager was launched in 1977 and its team is still keeping it going nearly half a century later. Building things that last, and keeping them running, is an underrated skill.

One from home

The 2026 Deloitte Tech Fast 50 is out, and Australian technology companies grew by an average of 481% over three years, with seven growing more than 1,000% (Deloitte). NSW's Stakk took top spot with growth of 2,993%. It's an AI-native business that helps organisations fight fraud through identity checks, document authentication and real-time decisions. Victoria's WorkflowMAX came second at 2,610%, and Mesoblast third at 1,937%. Queensland had its best result since the program began tracking locations, with 22% of the list. As Deloitte's Josh Tanchel put it, "What stands out is how quickly Australian technology companies are adapting."

Why it matters: a more selective investment market hasn't stopped Australian technology businesses growing. And it's no surprise that the fastest of all is in fraud and trust, the same theme as story 3.

Just for fun

In Shenzhen this week, 151 humanoid robots danced the same routine at the same time to set a Guinness World Record (Humanoid Daily). The target was 110. It was a staged show ahead of the APEC summit rather than a test of useful work (ELiA Robotics), but that doesn't seem to have bothered anyone.

Regular readers may remember that in Edition 3, 110 robots danced better than most of us. Three weeks later, the record has gone up by 41. Until this week, that would have been a perfect "we got dancing robots before GTA VI" moment. Now we'll have to find a new way to measure time.
