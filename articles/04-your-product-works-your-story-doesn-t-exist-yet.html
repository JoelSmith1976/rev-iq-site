Your Product Works. Your Story Doesn't Exist Yet.

A step-by-step way to define who the product is for, what it solves, and why now, so the market can finally understand what you built.

6 min readFree · email required

The product isn't the problem

A working product with no clear market story is a common state, not a rare failure. It happens to legacy SaaS that drifted feature by feature without a positioning decision, and it happens to founders who used AI tools to ship something functional in weeks but never had to articulate who it was for along the way.

The instinct in this spot is to keep building, assuming the story will become obvious once there are more features or more polish. It rarely does. Positioning is a decision, not a byproduct of development, and it has to be made deliberately or the product will keep sounding like it's for everyone, which in practice means it sounds like it's for no one.

Narrow the buyer before you touch the message

Every unclear pitch traces back to an unresolved question: who, specifically, is this for? Not "small businesses" or "teams that need efficiency" — a named role, at a named type of company, dealing with a named recurring problem. If the honest answer is "a lot of different people use it for different things,” that's not a strength to advertise, it's a sign the segment hasn't been chosen yet.

Choosing a narrow starting segment doesn't shrink the business — it gives the message something to grab onto. A specific buyer recognizes themselves in specific language. A generic buyer profile produces generic language that convinces nobody.

State the problem the way the buyer states it, not the way you built the solution

Founders tend to describe their product from the inside out — starting with the architecture or the clever mechanism — while buyers experience their problem from the outside in, as a cost, a risk, or a recurring annoyance. Positioning has to be written in the buyer's language, using the words they'd use to complain about the problem to a colleague, not the words used internally to describe the build.

If the problem statement doesn't produce a nod of recognition from someone in the target segment within one sentence, it isn't sharp enough yet. Vague problem statements are usually a sign the segment is still too broad.

Proof beats claims, even at zero revenue

Early-stage and legacy-stuck SaaS often default to adjective-heavy claims — "powerful," "seamless," "intuitive" — because they don't yet have case studies. But proof doesn't require a logo wall. A specific before/after scenario, a demo that shows the exact workflow being replaced, or a concrete number describing time or cost saved in a real (even internal or beta) use case all count as proof and outperform adjectives every time.

If there is truly zero usage data yet, the honest substitute is specificity about mechanism: exactly how the product does the thing, described plainly enough that a skeptical buyer can evaluate the claim themselves rather than being asked to trust it.

Pricing is part of the story, not an afterthought

How a product is priced signals who it's for and how it should be evaluated, before a buyer reads a single feature list. A per-seat price says "built for teams"; a flat fee says "built for one decision-maker"; usage-based pricing says "pay for value as you scale." A pricing model that contradicts the stated target segment (e.g., enterprise-style tiers pitched at solo operators) undercuts the whole narrative even if the product itself is solid.

Pricing framing doesn't need to be finalized to be useful in positioning — it needs to be consistent with the buyer, the problem, and the promise being made. Use the Positioning Canvas for This Product to lock those four pieces together before writing another line of marketing copy.

Why now is the sentence most teams skip

Buyers don't just ask "why this product," they ask "why deal with this problem today instead of continuing to ignore it." A credible "why now" points to something that changed recently — a cost that went up, a workaround that stopped scaling, a new expectation from customers or regulators — not a generic statement about the market growing.

Skipping this sentence is why technically sound products sit at zero revenue: the buyer understands what the product does but sees no urgency to act, and an unurgent problem gets deprioritized indefinitely.

Key takeaways
  • A working product with no clear story is a positioning gap, not a product gap.
  • Name a specific buyer before writing a single line of messaging.
  • Write the problem in the buyer's words, not the build's internal logic.
  • Concrete proof, even from one use case, beats adjective-heavy claims.
  • Pricing model and target segment must tell the same story, or neither is believable.
