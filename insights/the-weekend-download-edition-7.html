The Weekend Download, Edition 7

AI helps crack maths' most famous problem. Plus a US$43.9 billion cybersecurity deal, Nvidia's CEO returns, a million satellites get the green light and a message sent through a brick wall using gravity.

Week ending 28 November 20269 min readFree to read

Welcome back

Welcome to the seventh edition of The Weekend Download, Rev-IQ's Saturday round-up of the most remarkable things that happened in technology this week. One big story, five more, a number, one from home and something to make you smile, each with one line on why it matters if you run, build or back a technology business.

This week a 167-year-old maths problem may finally have fallen, and nobody is quite sure how. We start there, then move on to the biggest cybersecurity deal on record, a chief executive who was back at his desk 11 days after leaving it, a million satellites, an AI that designs proteins and a crater the size of a stadium.

The one everyone's talking about

AI helps crack the Riemann hypothesis

In 1859, the German mathematician Bernhard Riemann made a prediction about how prime numbers are spread out. It became the most famous unsolved problem in mathematics, and in 2000 it was named one of the Clay Mathematics Institute's Millennium Prize Problems, with US$1 million for whoever proved it (SBS News).

On Friday 20 November, Levent Alpöge, a mathematician at Anthropic, announced that he had used the company's AI, Claude, to produce a proof (Smithsonian). The proof isn't written like a normal maths paper. It's 17 million lines of code in Lean, a programming language that checks the logic of every step, and independent experts have since run their own checks on it (Smithsonian). Alpöge was generous about who did the heavy lifting: "Claude far outpaced me near the finish line" (Smithsonian).

The reaction from mathematicians has been a mix of awe and vertigo. Brian Conrey, one of the world's leading specialists on the problem, called it "a monumental moment in human history" (SBS News). Rutgers' Alex Kontorovich described "some kind of out-of-body experience" (SBS News). Only last month, Terence Tao had speculated that AI might not solve it before 2050 (New Scientist).

Here's the strange part. The consensus is that the proof will hold, but nobody, including Alpöge, yet understands how it works (New Scientist). "What Alpöge did is that he produced a formal proof that he does not understand," said NYU's Maksym Radziwiłł. "This certifies the statement as correct, but does not explain to us why it is correct" (Smithsonian). Kontorovich put it more simply: "At the moment, all we know is the car actually works" (Smithsonian).

Our part of the world was watching too. Associate Professor Melissa Humphries of Adelaide University told SBS that hundreds of existing proofs simply assume the hypothesis is true, and that once it is confirmed, those proofs "will all be much more concrete". She also stressed that it still needs to be written up as a conventional proof and checked by people (SBS News). That will take a while. Under the Clay Institute's rules, a solution has to be published in a peer-reviewed journal and survive two years of scrutiny before the prize can be awarded (SBS News).

It wasn't cheap, either. Kontorovich said he "would be shocked" if producing the proof cost less than US$10 million (Smithsonian).

Why it matters: New Scientist points out that maths and code are the fields where AI moves fastest, because answers can be checked instantly. Most business work can't be checked that easily (New Scientist). If you're putting AI into your product or your operations, the question to ask first isn't how clever the AI is. It's how you'll know when it's right.

The five

1. The biggest cybersecurity deal on record

On Monday, CrowdStrike agreed to buy Zscaler in an all-stock deal worth about US$43.9 billion, a 34% premium to Zscaler's share price (CrowdStrike via SEC). Reuters calls it the biggest cybersecurity acquisition on record (Reuters). CrowdStrike protects laptops, servers and cloud workloads, while Zscaler secures how staff connect to applications and data. Together they expect to start with more than US$10 billion in annual recurring revenue, and to give CrowdStrike a path to about 8,000 Zscaler customers who don't yet buy from it (CrowdStrike via SEC). The pitch to customers is "one platform, one data layer, and one contract" (CrowdStrike via SEC). The deal is expected to close in the second half of 2027 (Reuters).

Why it matters: buyers want fewer vendors and fewer contracts. If you sell a point solution, expect to be asked how you fit into somebody else's platform, and have a good answer ready before your customer's next renewal.

2. Nvidia's chief executive is back

Two weeks ago we reported that Jensen Huang had resigned as Nvidia's chief executive. This week he returned. Nvidia's board reinstated him on Monday 23 November, 11 days after he resigned amid a personal-conduct controversy (Reuters). Less than a week earlier, the chairman had said the company would not rehire him (Reuters). The company said the decision followed "input from senior levels of the United States government" (AP), and President Trump told CNBC his intervention was "very important" (Reuters). Investors liked it: Reuters reported the shares up 33%, on track to add more than US$900 billion in market value (Reuters). Governance experts were less impressed, and the board's independence is now under scrutiny (AP).

One business was simply pleased to have him back. Nvidia was famously dreamed up in a booth at a Denny's in San Jose, and the diner chain has confirmed that the plaque above it is back where it belongs. "Every story starts somewhere. We're proud to be part of Jensen's," it said (The New York Times).

Why it matters: when a founder is worth hundreds of billions of dollars to the share price in a single day, that's key-person risk at a scale few boards ever see. Most technology businesses have a smaller version of the same problem, and fewer people to catch it.

3. A million satellites, cleared for take-off

The US Federal Communications Commission has approved SpaceX's plan to launch up to a million satellites to run data centres in space, the first approval of its kind (BBC News). The constellation, called Starmind, would use sunlight to power satellites that process AI workloads and send the results back to Earth (BBC News). SpaceX has to start deploying by November 2033 and have 10% in orbit by 2035 (BBC News). Not everyone is convinced. The American Astronomical Society said it was "disheartened and dismayed", and satellite pioneer Sir Martin Sweeting said the idea seemed technically feasible, but it's not yet clear whether it "makes sense at scale" (BBC News).

Why it matters: demand for AI computing is now big enough that putting data centres in orbit is a licensed plan, not science fiction. Whatever happens to Starmind, the cost of AI compute is still the number to watch in your own margins.

4. An AI that designs proteins, then sees them built

OpenAI says its GPT-6 models can design new proteins using only reasoning and code, without relying on specialised biology models to produce the designs (OpenAI). Its lab partner, Onava, made 322 small proteins from designs by GPT-6.2 and tested whether they stuck to five target proteins. 35 of them, or 10.9%, did (OpenAI). When the model was asked to redesign parts of existing antibodies, about 70% of the tested designs still bound (OpenAI). OpenAI is clear about the limits: predicting a protein's full structure is still a weak spot, and the results still need to be independently replicated (R&D World).

Why it matters: a general-purpose model doing specialist work is the same pattern many software businesses are facing. If a general model can do what your specialist product does, your advantage has to come from somewhere else: your data, your workflow or your customers' trust.

5. A crater the size of a stadium

In 2022, NASA deliberately flew its DART spacecraft into a small asteroid called Dimorphos to see if it could change its orbit. It did, by 33 minutes, much more than predicted (ESA). This week the European Space Agency's Hera spacecraft sent back its first close-up pictures of the result. The face DART hit has been flattened into a crater "the size of a sports stadium" stretching across the whole asteroid, and Dimorphos is now "tumbling wildly in its orbit" (ESA). Scientists had debated whether the impact left a crater, changed the asteroid's shape or did nothing. "Much to our surprise the answer turns out to be all three at once," said Hera's principal investigator, Patrick Michel (ESA).

Why it matters: DART proved the idea worked. Hera is finding out what actually happened. Any change you make in a business deserves the same second look once the dust settles.

Number of the week: 1 bit per minute

That's how fast a team at the University of Queensland sent a message through a brick wall using gravity. They moved a 1950s lift and its 1.8-tonne counterweight between two floors to represent 0s and 1s, and a sensitive gravity meter in the bathroom next door picked up the tiny changes and decoded the word "Gravity" (Phys.org). "Our elevator was snail-paced," said Professor Andrew White, so one word took an hour (Phys.org). The appeal is that, unlike radio or Wi-Fi, a gravity signal can't be blocked by walls, rock or water. As Dr Charles Woffinden put it, "Anyone who has lost phone reception in a tunnel or struggled with Wi-Fi through a solid wall will understand the appeal" (Phys.org). The project started as an attempt to weigh trucks without them stopping (Phys.org).

Why it matters: one bit per minute is a terrible product and a fascinating proof of concept. Good ideas often look this unimpressive at the start.

One from home

A broadband cable under New Zealand's Southern Alps has turned out to be a world-class earthquake sensor. Working with Chorus, researchers from Singapore's NTU, the Australian National University, Victoria University of Wellington and the Chinese Academy of Sciences sent laser pulses down a spare strand of fibre running 30 kilometres along the Haast Pass highway, effectively turning it into thousands of sensors spaced four metres apart (NTU Singapore). Over 140 days of recording it picked up 67 local earthquakes, and revealed that the Alpine Fault isn't a single crack but a band of shattered rock about 200 metres wide (NTU Singapore). Its main slipping core runs unbroken for about 180 kilometres, which suggests two sections of the fault are connected at earthquake depth. The researchers say that doesn't change the projected timing of the next big quake, but it does sharpen planning for it (NTU Singapore). And customers' internet kept running the whole time (NTU Singapore).

Why it matters: the most valuable new use of an asset is often hiding in something you already own. Before you buy something new, check what your existing infrastructure, data or customer base could already tell you.

Just for fun

Last week we finally got GTA VI. This week, one player is driving it with his mind. Neuralink shared a video of a trial participant who can't use his arms piloting a speedboat, flying a seaplane and getting into a fistfight in the game, using his brain implant as the controller (PCMag). Before the implant, he played with "a mouth stick and an iPad", which he said left him "like a bird, pecking at an iPad". Now, he said, "you just immediately get your portal to the world back" (PCMag).

IGN's headline put it best: "We got Neuralink gameplay in GTA 6 before... wait" (IGN). We're still looking for a new way to measure time.
